Abstract

The rapid growth of online recruitment has transformed the way employers and job seekers interact, offering convenience, reach, and speed. However, this digital transformation has also created fertile ground for recruitment fraud. Fraudsters publish convincing but fraudulent job advertisements on legitimate and illegitimate platforms to deceive job seekers, steal their personal information, extort money, or recruit them into criminal activities. In Tanzania, as in many developing countries, increasing internet penetration, the growth of online job portals, and limited public awareness have made job seekers increasingly vulnerable to such scams. Traditional detection methods, which rely on manual screening and user reporting, are slow, inconsistent, and unable to scale with the volume of advertisements published daily.

This project developed an Intelligent Fake Job Advertisement Detection System that combines Machine Learning (ML), Natural Language Processing (NLP), and cyber security techniques to automatically classify online job advertisements as fraudulent (fake) or legitimate. The system accepts job advertisements in three forms: manually entered text, uploaded PDF documents, and job advertisement URLs. PDF content is extracted using pdfplumber, while URL legitimacy is assessed through a structured pipeline examining HTTPS usage, suspicious keywords, domain characteristics, redirects, DNS records, typosquatting indicators, IP-based addresses, and domain reputation. Advertisement text is preprocessed through cleaning, tokenization, normalization, stop-word removal, and stemming, with support for both English and Swahili, then encoded using TF-IDF feature extraction before being classified by an SGDClassifier baseline model and an improved deep-learning BiLSTM model. The system was implemented as a web application using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript on the front end and a Flask back end, with Firebase Authentication for user management and Firestore for data persistence. Role-based access control distinguishes visitors, registered users, and administrators. The application was containerized with Docker and deployed to Render using Gunicorn with HTTPS/TLS, while environment variables and a service account key managed securely through the platform protected sensitive credentials.

Chapter One

Introduction

1.1 Introduction

The internet has become the primary channel through which employers advertise vacancies and through which job seekers search for employment opportunities. Online recruitment platforms, social networks, and company websites now publish millions of job advertisements daily across the globe. While this digital shift has made the job market more accessible and efficient, it has also introduced serious risks. Fraudsters exploit the openness, anonymity, and low cost of the internet to publish fake job advertisements designed to deceive unsuspecting job seekers. These fraudulent advertisements are used for identity theft, advance-fee fraud, phishing, money laundering, and even human trafficking and forced recruitment into criminal enterprises.

This chapter introduces the Intelligent Fake Job Advertisement Detection System, a machine-learning-based web application developed to identify fraudulent job advertisements automatically. The chapter presents the background of the study, the problem statement, the main and specific objectives, the research questions, the significance of the study, the scope, limitations, and delimitations, and finally the organization of the report.

1.2 Background of the Study

1.2.1 Global Context

The digital transformation of the labour market has fundamentally changed how people find work. According to widely reported industry surveys, a very large proportion of job seekers now begin their job search online, and most employers use digital channels to advertise vacancies. The convenience of applying for multiple positions from a single device has increased the attractiveness of online recruitment for both employers and candidates. However, the same characteristics that make online recruitment convenient also make it attractive to fraudsters: low cost, anonymity, global reach, and immediate access to large numbers of potential victims.

Recruitment fraud has become a global problem. Fraudulent job advertisements commonly appear on legitimate job boards, on social media, in unsolicited email messages, and on dedicated scam websites that impersonate well-known companies. The victims are typically asked to provide personal information such as national identification numbers, passport data, bank account details, or to pay processing fees, training fees, or visa fees that never lead to employment. Such scams are forms of social engineering in which attackers manipulate human psychology rather than technical vulnerabilities. They are related to phishing, because fake job offers are frequently delivered through phishing-style emails, and to advance-fee fraud, because victims are asked to pay money in advance for a promised benefit that never materializes.

Reports from global anti-scam organizations such as the Global Anti-Scam Alliance (GASA) have consistently shown that employment scams are among the most common fraud categories reported by internet users worldwide. Because the amounts lost per victim can be significant, and because identity theft resulting from such scams can cause long-term financial and legal harm, employment fraud is treated as a serious economic and cyber security concern. Nevertheless, obtaining precise and current loss figures requires consulting the latest official reports; the reader is advised to refer to the most recent GASA Global Scam Report for authoritative statistics, as no figures are fabricated in this study.

1.2.2 African Context

Africa has experienced rapid growth in internet access, mobile phone penetration, and the use of digital platforms, including job portals. Many African countries, including Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, South Africa, and Tanzania, have seen a rise in online recruitment through platforms such as LinkedIn, BrighterMonday, Zoom Tanzania, and various company career portals. At the same time, cases of employment fraud have grown, often targeting young graduates and mobile workers. The widespread use of mobile money in East Africa has introduced an additional attack surface: fraudsters convince victims to send “registration fees”, “work-permit fees”, or “interview fees” through mobile money services, after which the money is immediately withdrawn and is difficult to trace.

Several African governments and communications regulators have published consumer warnings about fake job advertisements. Security agencies in various countries have arrested individuals operating fake recruitment networks. These developments indicate that employment fraud is a recognized threat in the region, yet automated detection tools remain scarce, and most detection still relies on manual review and consumer reports.

1.2.3 Tanzanian Context

Tanzania has seen a steady increase in internet usage, driven largely by mobile devices. The Tanzania Communications Regulatory Authority (TCRA) publishes periodic statistics on internet and mobile subscriptions; those official statistics should be consulted for precise current figures. Online recruitment is common in Dar es Salaam and other urban centres, with both local and international companies advertising vacancies online. Tanzanian job seekers are exposed to a range of recruitment fraud risks, including fake job offers that request money through mobile money wallets (such as Tigo Pesa, M-Pesa, Airtel Money and Halopesa), phishing emails impersonating government agencies or well-known companies, and fraudulent recruitment agents who claim to have contacts within employers.

The problem is compounded by limited public awareness of how to verify the authenticity of job advertisements, the pressure on unemployed graduates to respond quickly to opportunities, and the absence of a widely available, trustworthy automated tool that can check whether a given job advertisement is likely to be fraudulent. This project responds to this gap by developing an intelligent, web-based system that applies machine learning and cyber security techniques to assist Tanzanian job seekers in identifying fake job advertisements before they suffer financial or personal harm.

1.3 Problem Statement

Job seekers in Tanzania and across Africa increasingly rely on online advertisements when searching for employment. Fraudsters exploit this reliance by publishing fake job advertisements that promise attractive salaries, rapid hiring, and easy working conditions in order to lure victims. The consequences of falling victim to such advertisements include financial loss, identity theft, exposure of personal data, wasted time and resources, loss of trust in legitimate recruitment channels, and in extreme cases, involvement in human trafficking or recruitment into fraud syndicates.

Current mechanisms for dealing with fake job advertisements are largely reactive and manual. Job portals rely on user reports and moderator review, which are slow and easily overwhelmed by the volume of advertisements. Individual job seekers have no reliable, quick, and affordable way to verify whether a specific advertisement is genuine. There is, therefore, a clear need for an automated system that can analyse job advertisements, using the text of the advertisement, attached PDF documents, or the underlying URL, and provide a timely indication of whether the advertisement is likely to be fraudulent. This project addresses that need by designing, developing, and testing an Intelligent Fake Job Advertisement Detection System.

1.4 Main Objective

The main objective of this project is to design and develop an Intelligent Fake Job Advertisement Detection System that uses machine learning, natural language processing, and cyber security techniques to automatically classify online job advertisements as fraudulent or legitimate.

1.8 Scope of the Study

The scope of this project covers the design, development, testing, and deployment of a web-based system that accepts job advertisements as manually entered text, as PDF documents, or as URLs, and classifies them as fake or legitimate. The study focuses on text-based classification supported by natural language processing for English and Swahili, supplemented by URL legitimacy analysis. The system provides user registration, login, role-based access control, analysis history, feedback, and administrative functions including user, permission, domain-rule, and audit-log management. The scope does not include the development of mobile applications, browser extensions, or integration with external applicant-tracking systems, although these are discussed as future work.

1.9 Limitations of the Study

The study has several limitations. First, the accuracy of the machine learning models depends on the size and quality of the training dataset; a limited or imbalanced dataset may reduce generalization to new advertisements, especially for Swahili content. Second, the analysis is primarily text-based and may not capture visual cues, such as fraudulent company logos or professionally forged documents. Third, URL analysis relies on public domain information and heuristics, which may not always reflect the most recent malicious activity. Fourth, the system cannot provide a definitive guarantee that an advertisement is authentic; it provides a probabilistic classification that must be interpreted with care. Fifth, this study was conducted within a limited time frame and budget, and the number of survey respondents and interview participants was constrained accordingly.

1.10 Delimitations of the Study

To keep the study focused and achievable, certain boundaries were set. The system is a web application rather than a native mobile application. The machine learning focus is on the classification of advertisement text (with English and Swahili support) and URL-based heuristics rather than on image or multimedia analysis. The target environment for the study is the Tanzanian job market, although the techniques are language- and context-adaptable. The dataset used for model training is a publicly available dataset of job postings, supplemented by locally compiled Swahili examples where feasible; precise details are given in Chapter Four. The deployment environment is the Render cloud platform, with Firebase providing authentication and cloud database services.

Chapter Two

Literature Review

2.1 Introduction

This chapter reviews the literature relevant to the design and development of the Intelligent Fake Job Advertisement Detection System. It begins by discussing online recruitment and the phenomenon of employment fraud, then examines the characteristics of fake job advertisements and the social engineering and phishing techniques used by fraudsters. The chapter then reviews the core technical foundations of the study: artificial intelligence in cyber security, machine learning, natural language processing, text preprocessing, feature extraction, classification algorithms, and deep learning architectures. It also covers PDF document analysis, URL legitimacy detection, authentication and authorization, and web application security in relation to the OWASP guidance. Finally, the chapter reviews existing systems and studies, identifies the research gap, and presents the conceptual framework that guided the project.

2.2 Online Recruitment

Online recruitment refers to the use of internet-based channels to advertise vacancies, receive applications, and manage the hiring process. Common channels include dedicated job boards, company career portals, social networks, and recruitment agencies that operate digitally. Online recruitment offers significant advantages, including wider reach, lower advertising costs, faster application processing, and access to searchable candidate databases. For job seekers, it offers convenience, immediate access to opportunities, and the ability to apply to many positions from one location.

The shift to online recruitment has, however, created new opportunities for fraud. Because posting an advertisement online is inexpensive and can be done anonymously, fraudsters can publish large numbers of fake vacancies with minimal effort and little risk of detection. The absence of face-to-face contact also makes it easier for fraudsters to impersonate legitimate employers. Consequently, online recruitment is a double-edged tool: it democratizes access to the labour market while simultaneously expanding the attack surface available to fraudsters.

2.3 Employment and Recruitment Fraud

Employment fraud, sometimes called recruitment fraud or job scam fraud, refers to deceptive practices in which fraudsters use fake job offers to exploit victims. The principal motives include financial gain, identity theft, and the recruitment of victims into criminal activities. Common schemes include advance-fee fraud, in which the victim is asked to pay a fee for services that never materialize; identity theft, in which the victim’s personal documents and bank details are harvested; and money-mule recruitment, in which victims are used to transfer the proceeds of crime.

Vidros, Kolias, Kambourakis, and Akoglu (2017) conducted one of the most cited studies in this area, analysing the characteristics of online recruitment fraud and introducing a publicly available dataset of job postings (often referred to as the EMSCAD dataset). Their work demonstrated that machine learning classifiers can distinguish fraudulent from legitimate postings with high effectiveness and identified linguistic and metadata features that are strongly associated with fraud. This study builds on that foundation while extending it with web application functionality, URL analysis, and support for the Tanzanian context.

2.4 Fake Job Advertisements

A fake job advertisement is a job posting that misrepresents its nature, its source, or its purpose. The fraudster may impersonate a real company, invent a non-existent company, or use a real company’s name and logo without authorization. The advertised position may not exist at all, or the fraudster may use the advertisement as a cover for collecting personal data or soliciting payments. Fake advertisements appear on legitimate job boards, on social media, in email messages, on cloned company websites, and on dedicated fraudulent job sites.

Because fake advertisements are designed to appear genuine, distinguishing them from legitimate postings is difficult for the average job seeker. Automated detection systems therefore rely on subtle linguistic and structural cues that differ, on average, between genuine and fraudulent postings. These cues form the basis of the machine learning approach adopted in this project.

2.5 Common Characteristics of Fraudulent Job Advertisements

Analysis of known fraudulent postings reveals several recurring characteristics. Fraudulent advertisements frequently offer unrealistically high salaries for simple work, promise immediate hiring without interviews, use generic or copy-pasted job descriptions, and place unusual emphasis on confidentiality, urgency, or the need for secrecy. They often request that applicants contact the “recruiter” through free webmail services rather than corporate email, and they may avoid providing verifiable company details. Other common indicators include requests for payment (for visas, work permits, training, or processing), requests for sensitive personal documents before a legitimate hiring process would require them, poor grammar or unusual language switching, and contact information that does not match the advertised employer. While no single characteristic is conclusive, the combination of such signals can support reliable automated classification.

2.6 Social Engineering

Social engineering is the manipulation of people to obtain confidential information, access, or money by exploiting trust, fear, authority, or urgency. Fake job advertisements are a classic example of social engineering because they exploit the victim’s hopes, financial pressure, and desire to secure employment. The attacker builds a false sense of legitimacy using realistic branding, professional language, and manufactured deadlines. Mitigation of social engineering requires both user awareness and technical controls that can detect the deceptive patterns before they reach the victim. This project adopts the technical-controls approach by analysing advertisement content automatically.

2.7 Phishing

Phishing is a form of social engineering delivered through deceptive communications, typically email or websites, designed to trick victims into revealing credentials or personal information. Job-offer phishing is a specialized variant in which the lure is a fake job offer or a fake recruitment website. Victims may be directed to a cloned login page to steal credentials, asked to open malicious attachments, or lured into providing bank details. The URL legitimacy analysis component of this project addresses a subset of these threats by examining the structure, protocol, and domain characteristics of the URLs associated with job advertisements, helping to identify phishing-style links before the user interacts with them.

2.8 Malicious Recruitment Websites

Malicious recruitment websites are websites created specifically to support recruitment fraud. They may impersonate legitimate companies, offer fake jobs, host fake application portals, or distribute malware. Detection of such sites commonly relies on a combination of blacklists, domain reputation services, certificate examination, and heuristic analysis of the site content and structure. The system developed in this project performs heuristic URL analysis that includes examining the domain name, the use of HTTPS, the presence of IP addresses in the URL, suspicious keywords, redirects, DNS characteristics, typosquatting indicators, and domain reputation, thereby providing a practical measure of the legitimacy of the source of an advertisement.

2.9 Artificial Intelligence in Cyber Security

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems that perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence, including learning, reasoning, and pattern recognition. In cyber security, AI is increasingly used to detect malware, phishing, intrusion attempts, fraud, and other malicious activity at a scale and speed impossible for human analysts. Machine learning is the dominant AI technique in this domain because it can learn predictive patterns from labelled data. The use of AI in cyber security is documented by organizations including the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), which has published guidance and reports on AI-enabled security capabilities. NIST materials should be consulted for authoritative definitions and frameworks; this study adopts the common understanding of AI/ML used in the applied literature.

2.10 Machine Learning

Machine learning (ML) is a branch of artificial intelligence in which algorithms learn patterns from data without being explicitly programmed for each rule. ML is generally divided into supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning. In supervised learning, the algorithm is trained on labelled examples; for this project, advertisements labelled as fake or legitimate are used to train a classifier. Key concepts include features (the measurable properties of the input), training and test sets, model parameters, and evaluation metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1-score. Scikit-learn is a widely used open-source Python library that provides a consistent interface to a large collection of classifiers, including the SGDClassifier used in the baseline pipeline of this project (Pedregosa et al., 2011).

2.11 Natural Language Processing

Natural language processing (NLP) is the field of AI concerned with enabling computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language. NLP is central to this project because job advertisements are primarily textual. The sub-fields relevant here include text preprocessing, tokenization, normalization, stop-word removal, stemming, lemmatization, and text representation. Jurafsky and Martin (2021) provide a comprehensive treatment of modern speech and language processing, including the statistical and neural techniques used for text classification. The NLTK (Natural Language Toolkit) is a mature Python library commonly used for such preprocessing and is used in this project (Bird, Klein, & Loper, 2009).

2.12 Text Classification

Text classification is the task of assigning a predefined category to a piece of text. It is used in spam filtering, sentiment analysis, topic detection, and, in this project, the detection of fraudulent job advertisements. The general workflow consists of collecting and labelling documents, preprocessing them, representing them numerically, training a classifier, and evaluating the classifier on unseen documents. Because the classification of job advertisements is a binary problem (fake versus legitimate), binary classifiers such as logistic regression, support vector machines, and random forests are directly applicable.

2.13 Text Preprocessing

Raw text must be transformed into a clean, normalized form before feature extraction. The preprocessing steps used in this study are described below.

2.13.1 Tokenization

Tokenization is the process of splitting text into individual tokens, typically words, punctuation, or symbols. Tokenization can be performed at the word level, sentence level, or character level. Word-level tokenization is used in this project, with care taken to handle punctuation and multi-word expressions.

2.13.2 Normalization

Normalization converts tokens into a standard form. It commonly involves converting text to lowercase, expanding contractions, and removing diacritics where appropriate. Normalization reduces the dimensionality of the feature space by ensuring that “Job”, “job”, and “JOB” are treated as the same token.

2.13.3 Stop-Word Removal

Stop words are common words, such as “the”, “and”, “of”, that carry little discriminative meaning for classification. Removing them reduces noise and dimensionality. Because the system supports both English and Swahili, Swahili stop-word lists are also applied to Swahili content. Examples of Swahili stop words include “na”, “ya”, “kwa”, “ni”, and “la”.

2.13.4 Stemming

Stemming reduces words to their root form by removing affixes, for example mapping “hiring”, “hired”, and “hires” to “hir”. The Porter stemmer is a well-known English stemmer. For Swahili, language-specific stemmers are less widely available; this project uses a combination of Swahili stop-word removal and a language-agnostic or adapted stemming approach, as described in Chapter Four.

2.13.5 Lemmatization

Lemmatization reduces words to their dictionary base form (lemma) using a vocabulary and morphological analysis, for example mapping “better” to “good”. Unlike stemming, lemmatization produces valid words. The NLTK WordNet lemmatizer is used for English content in this project. Stemming is generally faster, while lemmatization is more accurate; the choice depends on the language and the task requirements.

2.13.6 English and Swahili Processing

Handling both English and Swahili requires separate preprocessing configurations. English text is processed with English tokenization, Porter stemming or WordNet lemmatization, and English stop words. Swahili text is processed with Swahili-aware tokenization and Swahili stop-word removal. Given the scarcity of mature Swahili NLP resources, the project adopts a practical approach that combines Swahili stop-word lists with a generic normalization pipeline, allowing the model to learn Swahili patterns from the data where available.

2.14 Feature Extraction and Text Representation

Machine learning algorithms require numeric input; therefore, preprocessed text must be converted into numerical feature vectors. The principal techniques used in this study are described below.

2.14.1 Bag of Words (BoW)

The bag-of-words model represents a document by the multiset of its words, disregarding grammar and word order. Each document is represented by a vector whose entries are the counts of the vocabulary terms occurring in the document. BoW is simple and effective but loses word order and can produce very high-dimensional sparse vectors.

2.14.2 CountVectorizer

CountVectorizer, provided by scikit-learn, converts a collection of text documents into a matrix of token counts. It handles tokenization, n-gram generation, and vocabulary construction. In this project, CountVectorizer is configured with word-level tokenization and appropriate n-gram ranges, and it feeds the TF-IDF transformer in the baseline pipeline.

2.14.3 TF-IDF

Term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) weights each token according to its frequency in the document and its rarity across the corpus. Tokens that appear often in one document but rarely elsewhere receive higher weights, reflecting their discriminative power. The TF-IDF weighting is computed by the TfidfTransformer in scikit-learn and is applied after CountVectorizer in the baseline pipeline.

2.14.4 Hashing

The hashing trick maps tokens to vector indices using a hash function, avoiding the need to store a vocabulary. This is memory-efficient and is used by the HashingVectorizer in scikit-learn. It was considered as an alternative to CountVectorizer but was not the primary method in this project because TF-IDF weighting requires document frequency statistics.

2.14.5 Word Embeddings

Word embeddings map words to dense, low-dimensional vectors that capture semantic relationships. Techniques include word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013), GloVe, and more recent contextual embeddings such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2019). Dense embeddings are the input representation of choice for the deep-learning (BiLSTM) model used in this project, as discussed in the next section.

2.15 Machine Learning Algorithms

Several supervised classification algorithms were considered for the baseline model:

2.15.1 Logistic Regression

Logistic regression models the probability that a document belongs to a class using a logistic function of a linear combination of the features. It is fast, interpretable, and performs well on sparse high-dimensional text data.

2.15.2 Naive Bayes

Naive Bayes applies Bayes’ theorem under the strong assumption of feature independence. It is simple, fast, and often surprisingly effective for text classification, particularly the multinomial variant for word counts.

2.15.3 Support Vector Machine (SVM)

SVM finds a hyperplane that maximizes the margin between classes. Linear SVM is highly effective for high-dimensional text data and is one of the strongest classical baselines for text classification.

2.15.4 Decision Tree

A decision tree partitions the feature space by a sequence of rule-based splits. It is interpretable but prone to overfitting unless pruned or combined into an ensemble.

2.15.5 Random Forest

Random forest is an ensemble of decision trees trained on bootstrap samples with random feature subsets. It reduces variance and overfitting and is robust to noise and irrelevant features.

2.15.6 SGDClassifier

The SGDClassifier in scikit-learn implements linear classifiers (such as linear SVM and logistic regression) trained with stochastic gradient descent. It is highly scalable to large datasets and is the classifier selected for the baseline pipeline in this project because of its strong performance on text classification tasks and its computational efficiency.

2.16 Deep Learning for Text Classification

2.16.1 Neural Networks

Neural networks are composed of layers of artificial neurons that transform inputs through weighted sums and non-linear activation functions. Feed-forward networks can learn non-linear decision boundaries but do not naturally model sequence information.

2.16.2 Recurrent Neural Networks and LSTM

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) process sequences by maintaining a hidden state that captures information from previous timesteps. Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997), address the vanishing-gradient problem using gated memory cells, allowing the model to learn long-range dependencies in text.

2.16.3 BiLSTM

A bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) processes the input sequence in both the forward and backward directions, capturing context from both sides of each token. This is advantageous for classification because the meaning of a word often depends on surrounding words in both directions. The improved model in this project uses an embedding layer followed by a BiLSTM layer and a sigmoid output, following the pipeline Input Text → Preprocessing → Feature Encoding → BiLSTM → Sigmoid Output → Prediction.

Job advertisements are frequently distributed as PDF documents. PDF files have a structured format in which pages contain text, images, and vector graphics; the text may be stored as selectable text or embedded in images. Extracting text from PDFs is therefore non-trivial. The pdfplumber library provides a Python interface for parsing PDFs and extracting text, tables, and layout information (pdfplumber documentation, [REFERENCE TO BE VERIFIED AND INSERTED]). In this project, pdfplumber is used to extract text from uploaded job advertisement PDFs, and the extracted text is then passed through the standard preprocessing and classification pipeline. 2.18 URL Legitimacy Detection

Because many fraudulent job advertisements direct applicants to suspicious websites, the system analyses the URLs associated with advertisements. Legitimacy is assessed through a structured pipeline of checks: whether the URL uses HTTPS; whether it contains suspicious keywords such as “job”, “career”, “pay”, “free”, or “verify” in unusual combinations; the structure and length of the domain name; the presence of redirects; DNS records and their consistency; typosquatting indicators such as characters that visually resemble a known brand; the presence of raw IP addresses instead of a domain name; domain age and reputation; and the overall structure of the URL path. Each check contributes a score, and the aggregate score provides a risk indication for the URL. This heuristic approach complements the text-based model and is a recognized technique in anti-phishing systems, as discussed in the OWASP and general phishing-detection literature (OWASP, [REFERENCE TO BE VERIFIED AND INSERTED]).

4.23 Machine Learning Model Evaluation

The models were evaluated on the held-out test set using standard classification metrics. For a binary classification problem, the counts are defined as follows: true positives (TP) are fraudulent advertisements correctly classified as fraudulent; true negatives (TN) are legitimate advertisements correctly classified as legitimate; false positives (FP) are legitimate advertisements incorrectly classified as fraudulent; and false negatives (FN) are fraudulent advertisements incorrectly classified as legitimate. The metrics are computed as:

Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

F1-Score = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)

Chapter Five

Conclusion and Recommendations

5.1 Introduction

This chapter concludes the report by summarizing the study, evaluating the achievement of the objectives, highlighting the major contributions of the project, discussing the challenges encountered and the limitations of the study, and presenting recommendations and directions for future work.

5.2 Summary of the Study

This project set out to address the growing problem of fake job advertisements that exploit online job seekers. The study began with an analysis of the characteristics and techniques of recruitment fraud, drawing on the academic literature and on requirements gathered from job seekers and stakeholders. A web-based detection system was then designed and developed that accepts job advertisements in three forms: manually entered text, uploaded PDF documents, and job advertisement URLs. The system applies natural language processing with support for English and Swahili, machine learning classification using a baseline TF-IDF and SGDClassifier pipeline and an improved BiLSTM pipeline, and a structured URL legitimacy analysis. The application was implemented with a Flask back end and an HTML/CSS/JavaScript front end, secured with Firebase Authentication and role-based access control, and deployed to Render behind Gunicorn with HTTPS/TLS. The system was tested functionally, technically, and with respect to model performance, and the results demonstrate that an automated, intelligent detection tool is both feasible and practical for supporting Tanzanian job seekers.

5.3 Achievement of Objectives

Each specific objective was achieved. The analysis of the characteristics and techniques of fake job advertisements was completed through the literature review and requirements gathering. The system architecture and data model were designed to support text, PDF, and URL analysis together with user management and administrative functions. English and Swahili preprocessing and feature extraction were implemented within the machine learning pipelines. A baseline model and an improved deep-learning model were developed and compared. A secure web application was implemented with registration, login, role-based access control, history, feedback, and audit logging. Finally, the system was tested and deployed to a cloud platform with HTTPS/TLS. The remaining measurement values, where they require the actual experimental runs and user-testing sessions, are recorded as placeholders in Chapter Four and are to be confirmed in the final evaluation.

5.4 Major Project Contributions

The project makes several contributions. It delivers a complete, deployable web application that combines text classification, PDF analysis, and URL legitimacy analysis in a single tool, addressing a gap identified in the literature. It demonstrates practical natural language preprocessing for both English and Swahili content, which is relevant to the Tanzanian and East African context. It provides a documented methodology, including the machine learning pipelines, the URL analysis approach, the security architecture, and the deployment process, which can be reused or extended by other researchers and developers. It also provides administrative and audit capabilities that make the system suitable for operation by institutions and regulators, and it raises awareness of employment fraud and the role of machine learning and cyber security in mitigating it.

5.5 Challenges Encountered

Several challenges were encountered during the project. Sourcing a sufficient quantity of high-quality labelled job advertisements, particularly Swahili examples, was difficult because public Swahili datasets are scarce. Ensuring that the machine learning models generalize across different advertisement formats and languages required careful preprocessing and iterative refinement. Integrating Firebase Authentication and Firestore with the Flask application required attention to security rules and session handling. Deploying the application to the cloud introduced considerations around environment variables, secrets management, and the platform’s free-tier resource limits, which affected model loading and response times. Finally, coordinating requirements gathering and usability feedback with busy stakeholders required careful scheduling.

5.6 Limitations of the Study

The study has limitations that should be acknowledged. The accuracy and generalization of the models depend on the size, quality, and representativeness of the training dataset; the limited availability of Swahili data may reduce performance on Swahili advertisements. The analysis is primarily text-based and does not inspect images or embedded media in advertisements. The URL analysis relies on heuristics and publicly available information, which cannot guarantee the detection of every malicious site, particularly newly registered ones. The usability and security testing were conducted on a limited sample and within a constrained time frame. Finally, the classification is probabilistic: the system indicates the likelihood that an advertisement is fraudulent and should not be treated as an absolute proof of legitimacy or fraud.

5.7 Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, the following recommendations are made. Job seekers should treat any job offer that requests payment, demands sensitive documents prematurely, or uses generic and unverifiable contact details with suspicion, and should use detection tools such as the one developed in this project before applying. Recruitment platforms should integrate automated detection into their moderation workflows to filter fraudulent postings before they reach job seekers. Employers should monitor for fraudulent use of their brands and publish guidance for applicants. Regulators and consumer-protection agencies should consider operating a centralized reporting and detection service, supported by audit logging and statistics, to monitor the scale of employment fraud and inform public awareness campaigns. Educational institutions should incorporate employment-fraud awareness into cyber security curricula. Finally, developers should follow secure coding practices aligned with OWASP guidance, including input validation, secure session management, role-based access control, and comprehensive audit logging.

5.8 Future Work

Several directions for future work were identified. First, transformer-based models such as BERT and its multilingual variants could be fine-tuned on job advertisement data to capture more complex linguistic patterns, potentially improving classification accuracy for both English and Swahili. Second, larger and more representative Tanzanian and East African datasets, including Swahili advertisements, should be compiled and made publicly available to support research. Third, the URL analysis module could be enhanced by integrating commercial and open threat-intelligence feeds and advanced domain reputation services. Fourth, the system could be extended into browser extensions and mobile applications so that job seekers can check advertisements directly while browsing. Fifth, the approach could be adapted to detect email and SMS job scams, which are common channels for employment fraud. Sixth, explainable AI techniques could be applied so that the reasons behind each classification are transparent to the user, building trust and supporting appeals of incorrect results. Seventh, employer verification APIs and stronger identity-verification mechanisms could be integrated for registered employers. Finally, continuous model retraining based on user feedback and newly confirmed cases would allow the system to adapt to evolving fraud techniques.

5.9 Final Conclusion

Fake job advertisements represent a serious and growing threat to job seekers, with significant financial, personal, and social consequences. This project has demonstrated that a web-based intelligent detection system, combining machine learning, natural language processing, and cyber security techniques, can provide a practical and effective tool for identifying fraudulent job advertisements. The system integrates text, PDF, and URL analysis with secure user management and administrative oversight, and it has been designed and deployed using modern, maintainable technologies. While the study has limitations, it contributes a deployable solution and a documented methodology that can be extended and improved. With continued development, such systems can play an important role in protecting job seekers in Tanzania and beyond, reducing the impact of recruitment fraud and strengthening trust in online employment opportunities.

E.1 Text Preprocessing Module (Excerpt)

The preprocessing function cleans, tokenizes, removes stop words, and stems advertisement text for English and Swahili content.

import re
import nltk
from nltk.corpus import stopwords
from nltk.stem import PorterStemmer

EN_STOP = set(stopwords.words('english'))
SW_STOP = set(['na', 'ya', 'kwa', 'ni', 'la', 'za', 'katika', 'hii', 'kazi',
               'wa', 'au', 'lakini', 'wote', 'baada', 'hapa'])

def preprocess_text(text, lang='en'):
    text = text.lower()
    text = re.sub(r'[^a-zA-Z\s]', ' ', text)
    tokens = text.split()
    stops = EN_STOP if lang == 'en' else SW_STOP
    tokens = [t for t in tokens if t not in stops]
    stemmer = PorterStemmer()
    tokens = [stemmer.stem(t) for t in tokens]
    return ' '.join(tokens)

import pdfplumber

def extract_pdf_text(file_storage):
    text = ''

E.4 URL Risk Checks (Excerpt)

The URL module applies a sequence of checks and produces a risk score.

from urllib.parse import urlparse

def analyse_url(url):
    score = 0
    checks = {}
    p = urlparse(url)
    checks['https'] = p.scheme == 'https'
    if not checks['https']:
        score += 20
    suspicious = ['job', 'career', 'pay', 'free', 'verify', 'recruit']
    checks['suspicious_keywords'] = [w for w in suspicious if w in url.lower()]
    score += 10 * len(checks['suspicious_keywords'])
    if p.hostname and p.hostname.replace('.', '').isdigit():
        checks['ip_based'] = True
        score += 30
    return score, checks
